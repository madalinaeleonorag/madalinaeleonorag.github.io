Assignment 1: Apr 2020 - Oct 2021

E-commerce platform & CMS — 6 grocery brands, Albertsons

One AEM-based CMS driving six brand websites for a major US grocery group, built by roughly 40 developers in Romania working alongside a similar number on the client side. I was on the product-browsing and cart team: product lists and carousels, add-to-cart, the product detail page, and the mini-cart in the site header.

  • Built roughly 90% of the coupon experience, the feature shoppers touched most. On a search or category page a product with eligible offers shows an "x coupons available" link; opening it slides in the offers with their terms ("20% off when you buy three"), and applying one strikes through the original price and shows the new one beside it. The same control repeats on the product page and inside the mini-cart, where applying a coupon turns green with a check, updates the order total in real time, and reports how many products the offer covers. Coupons could also be redeemed by code from the site or from the customer’s profile. The same machinery carried BOGO (buy one, get one) offers, where applying the code added a second unit at no charge.
  • Store-location logic driving stock availability, delivery pricing and in-store pickup — the omnichannel behaviour that decides what a customer is even allowed to order.
  • Everything shipped behind feature toggles controlled by the advertising side, which meant most components existed as two live versions at once and both had to work. It doubles the surface to maintain, which is why a toggle’s lifetime matters.
  • Separate applications that met in the browser. I used to call this a micro-frontend team, but it predates Module Federation — teams owned separate applications that integrated through shared browser storage rather than through a runtime contract. The modern term implies infrastructure we did not have.
  • One CMS drove six brands, so a change landed everywhere simultaneously and a bug could reproduce on one brand and not another. Working out the blast radius before changing anything was the daily discipline.
  • Wrote some functionality in plain JavaScript rather than Angular, either because it was a small AEM-authored component or because it was simply the leanest option: US shoppers on poor connections had to get a working page regardless of signal. Performance was tracked in the network panel, on total payload and CSS and JavaScript weight.
  • Accessibility was client-mandated and independently verified: ARIA, keyboard navigation and screen-reader behaviour (VoiceOver) were checked by testers on every ticket. Externally verified, not self-assessed.
  • Helped diagnose a production checkout outage: another team had given a CSS class the same name as one of ours, style encapsulation stopped holding, and a button disappeared on one brand’s site, so shoppers could not pay for around an hour. I was on call, and found the cause working through it with the senior developers. Scoped styles and token contracts are a correctness problem, not a tidiness one.
  • Also delivered a personalised reorder feature, real-time discount feedback with animations, daily build ownership, and feature demos for stakeholders.
  • Mentored one to two junior developers and wrote the learning plan for an IBM iX intern, onboarded new joiners into the wider IBM iX practice, and was the point of contact for the client’s US team, working US hours from Romania for the duration. Separately, taught a colleague enough Vue.js to start a new project in it, and contributed to a web-analytics project by reviewing colleagues’ code and building reusable components.

Assignment 2: Mar 2019 - Mar 2020

Munich Re — offline tablet app, document platform & device compliance

Three applications for a reinsurance client, learning Angular from close to nothing at the start of it. The main one was a tablet application used by repair technicians in customers’ homes, on connections that could not be relied on.

  • One of seven to eight developers on the tablet application: a technician arrives at a customer’s house, checks whether the appliance is covered, pulls up the policy details, records what was repaired, photographs the proof, and captures a customer signature that has to be legally valid. All of it has to work with no connection and reconcile later. The offline storage-and-sync layer was designed by our UI architect, not by me — I built against it rather than building it, and I would not claim to know its internals.
  • A demanding designer, which was the point. The client had their own designer who set a high bar and gave us genuinely difficult designs to build. Working to that standard, a design was a specification, not a suggestion.
  • Built the frontend for a company-wide document platform — every document the company held, research included, well over 10,000 of them, searchable by metadata and by content inside the files. Filtering ran on the backend; I built the interface over it. I also designed it entirely, with small inputs from the lead, which makes this the earliest thing I owned the look and behaviour of as well as the code.
  • Took over an ageing device-compliance application (Jamf-based) that checked enrolled company phones — weak passwords included. It already existed and had aged badly, so the work was refactoring it and remediating its dependency vulnerabilities, which I did by hand, package by package. It took a long time and there was no clever way to do it.
  • Three on-site trips to Munich, three to five days each: development discussions, demos, face-to-face time with the product owner and the client, and working alongside the Munich Re developers. I never watched a technician use the app in the field, but we had the devices and tested on them.
  • Unit tests on all of it — every application I worked on here had them.

Technologies

Assignment 3: Dec 2018 - Feb 2019

Internship — learning Angular and joining the client team

Started with training in Angular, Ionic and the rest of the stack. By the second or third month I was picking up small tickets on the Munich Re tablet application and taking part in the daily calls — so the internship and the role that followed are one continuous piece of work on the same project.

  • Learned Angular here, from close to zero. No JavaScript at that point, only Vue.js projects from student competitions and some courses. Angular and Ionic both came from this period.
  • Ended the internship with an offer already signalled: the feedback I had been given said the client was satisfied with the work I had done, and I moved onto a permanent contract on the same engagement.
